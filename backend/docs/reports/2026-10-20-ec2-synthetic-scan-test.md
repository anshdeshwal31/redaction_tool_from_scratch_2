# EC2 test on synthetic scanned documents (2026-10-20)

The tool was given six fictional scanned medico-legal documents (two easy, two medium, two hard) and
asked to redact them under Joel's policy. Each result was checked against an answer key that lists
every personal detail that must be replaced and everything that must stay.

- Code tested: commit `736ea90` (main), client profile. Self-check passed: 8 models, 17 data files.
- Machine: the `mlredact-dev` instance (m7i.xlarge, 4 vCPU, 16 GiB), one document at a time.
- Documents: generated by `backend/tools/synthdocs/corpus.py`; answer keys next to each PDF.
- Scoring: `backend/tools/synthdocs/score.py`, reading the redacted pages back with two text readers
  (PP-OCR and Tesseract). Documents the tool refused to release were analysed from the tool's sealed
  evaluator record instead (what it detected and what it replaced each detail with).
- Raw results: `backend/out/synth_runs_ec2_2026-10-20/` (git-ignored): redacted PDFs, the tool's
  records, the scorer's `runs/report/report.md`, and `diag/*/diagnosis.json` for the held-back documents.

## Results at a glance

| Document | Tier | Pages | Time | Outcome | Personal details replaced | Still readable (leaks) | Wrongly removed or changed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| GP referral letter | low | 1 | 2.5 min | released | 25 of 25 | 0 | 1 (a medicine) |
| Solicitor's instruction letter | low | 2 | 3.6 min | released | 26 of 28 | 2 organisation names | 4 (suburb, postcode, a date, a case citation) |
| IME report | medium | 3 | 6.4 min | **held back** by its own safety check | 32 of 33 found | 1 would have leaked | 3 ("banks", "L4/5", the word "Claim") |
| Hand-filled certificate of capacity | medium | 2 | 4.8 min | released | 18 of 18 | 0 | suburb, postcode, the diagnosis word "strain", form title, checkboxes |
| Psychiatric report | hard | 4 | 8.9 min | **held back** by its own safety check | 47 of 49 found | 2 would have leaked | suburb, postcode, "hope", a header, garbled text |
| Car-accident (CTP) claim bundle | hard | 3 | 5.4 min | **held back** by its own safety check | 46 of 51 found | up to 3 would have leaked | 2 suburbs, 2 postcodes, form labels; 2 pages would have been wiped |

Peak memory was under 3.7 GB for every document. About 15 seconds of each run is start-up; roughly
80% of the rest is spent reading the pages (about 2 minutes per page on this machine).

**What went well.** Everything the tool released was free of the personal details it *found*, and it
found a lot: every person, phone number, Medicare number, claim number and address in the GP letter;
every handwritten name and number on the certificate; in the hard documents, maiden names, children's
first names, initials, vehicle registrations, a VIN it misread (O for 0) yet still caught, the tax file
number, Centrelink number, bank account, ABN and ACN. Sideways and upside-down pages were read.
Stand-ins are consistent within a document in most places ("Dr Lee" became "Dr Gordon" wherever "Lisa
Gordon" appears), titles like "Mr" and "Dr" are kept, dates and ages are kept, and every page carries a
"De-identified copy" notice. When the tool doubted its own output it refused to release it. That is
the right instinct, but here it fired for the wrong reasons (issue 1).

## The issues, one at a time

### 1. Half the documents were held back by false alarms

The tool's last step re-checks its own output. If anything looks unsafe, it refuses to release the
document. It did this for three of the six, and in all three the "problem" was a side effect of a
small mistake elsewhere, not an actual leak.

- **IME report: the word "Claim" was mistaken for a company.** The tool replaced "Claim" (as in
  "Claim number") with a made-up company name. Its safety check then searched the output for the word
  "claim", found it inside "Claimant", and concluded a removed value had survived.
- **Car-accident bundle: a postcode was removed in one place and kept in another.** In the handwritten
  postal address the tool replaced the postcode 2111 together with the street. On the physiotherapy
  letterhead it kept 2111, which is correct, because the policy keeps postcodes. The safety check then saw a
  "removed" value still in the document.
- **Psychiatric report: punctuation was copied into reused stand-ins.** When a hospital is mentioned
  several times, the tool reuses one made-up name for all mentions, which is right. But the first mention was
  "Lakeside Private Hospital." with a full stop, so every later mention also got "Meridian Private
  Hospital." with a full stop. The safety check saw "Hospital." as a made-up word, noticed it is
  almost identical to the real word "Hospital", and flagged a stand-in that "looks like a real value".
  This happened twice for the hospital and once for "Return2Work Partners": the three clashes it reported.

**Why it matters:** none of these documents can be shown or delivered, including both hard ones.
**What to change:** have the safety check ignore matches that sit inside a longer word, and values
that also legitimately appear as kept text. Strip punctuation before reusing a stand-in. Never fold a
suburb or postcode into a replaced street when the policy keeps them (see issue 3).

### 2. Some organisation names and nicknames were missed

These are the only real privacy failures in the test.

- **Solicitor's letter (released):** "Queensland Medicolegal Assessments" and "Moreton Imaging" are
  still readable, and also still in the PDF's copyable text.
- **IME report (held back):** "Altona Aquatic Centre". The tool noticed it but judged the evidence too
  weak to act on.
- **Car-accident bundle (held back):** the insurer "Austral Mutual CTP" (also noticed and left alone),
  the physiotherapy practice name in a letterhead, and a handwritten employee number.
- **Psychiatric report (held back):** "Kellie", inside a quoted text message ("you're useless
  Kellie"), and the nickname "Kel" in the body text. The handwritten "Kel" was caught.

**Why it happens:** organisation names without a giveaway word such as "Pty Ltd", "Lawyers" or
"Hospital" get too little evidence. Short forms of a person's name aren't linked to the full name
("Kellie-Anne O'Sullivan"), so on their own they look like ordinary words.
**What to change:** treat runs of capitalised words ending in words like Centre, Imaging,
Assessments, Mutual or Physiotherapy as organisations. Once a person is known, also replace the
obvious short forms of their first name.

### 3. Suburbs and postcodes inside addresses were replaced

The policy keeps suburbs and postcodes and replaces only the street line. In four of the six documents, at
least one address lost its suburb or postcode: Brisbane 4000 (solicitor's letterhead), Blacktown 2148
(certificate), Newcastle 2300 (psychiatrist's letterhead), Gladesville 2111 and Balmain 2041
(car-accident forms). In the solicitor's letter Brisbane became "Wellington", a New Zealand city.
Simple addresses in the body of a letter were handled correctly ("Asquith NSW 2077" stayed).

**Why it happens:** when an address runs on into other things on the same line (a phone number in a
letterhead, a handwritten box), the tool treats the whole run as one street address and replaces all
of it. In one case it even labelled an employer's address as an employee number.
**What to change:** after finding an address, split off the suburb, state and postcode and keep
them. This matches what the policy says, and it also removes the cause of one of the false alarms in
issue 1.

### 4. Ordinary words and medical or legal content were changed

The tool replaced things that are not personal at all:

- **Medical content:** "Metformin XR 1 g" became "Xpnbfyfhn DF 6 b" (taken for an ID number);
  "L4/5" (a spinal level) became "I5/8"; the word "strain" in a handwritten diagnosis was blacked out.
- **A case citation:** "Malec v JC Hutton Pty Ltd (1990) 169 CLR 638" became "Malec v TIDEWATER Pty
  Ltd (1990) 802 DIT 242". Court cases are public and must stay exactly as written.
- **Words that look like names:** "hope" in "lost all hope" (the psychologist is Ms Hope), "banks" in
  "creek banks" (the claimant is Mr Banks), "née" (became "emily"), the header "PSYCHIATRIC REPORT"
  (became "NGOC MARSHALL").
- **Form labels and job words:** "Claim", "Claims Officer", "Employer" (so "Employer phone" became
  "Tidewater phone"), "Barista", "Pre-injury", "BSB", "IRN".
- **Garbled text:** on the sideways table page, the reader produced nonsense for a line of
  abbreviations, and the tool replaced the nonsense with made-up names.
- **A date lost its day:** "3 December 2024" lost the "3" when the clinic name in front of it was
  replaced.

**Why it happens:** the name and organisation detectors lean on capital letters and position, so
headings, labels and capitalised medical terms look like names. There is no list of words that are
never personal, such as form labels, medical terms and citations.
**What to change:** add a "never personal" list (form labels, job titles, medical abbreviations,
spinal levels, drug names with strengths). Recognise case citations ("X v Y (year) volume REPORT
page") and keep them. Never replace a single common English word that matches a person's surname
when it appears in lower case.

### 5. Colour and aged scans confuse the picture detector

The tool looks for stamps, signatures, handwriting and logos by colour and shape. On colour scans it
found far too many "stamps": 44 in the IME report, which has one real stamp, 18 in the
car-accident bundle and 8 in the certificate. On the certificate, the only colour document that was
released, you can see the damage:

- The title "WORKERS COMPENSATION" has grey blocks punched through it, and so does "Part D".
- Empty checkboxes became filled grey squares, so the page now looks as if both "Yes" and "No" were
  ticked. That changes the meaning of the form.
- Signatures became flat grey boxes instead of the stand-in pen strokes the policy asks for, because a
  false "stamp" overlapped them.
- The practice stamp became a large grey block with stand-in text printed messily on top.

On the car-accident bundle, which is yellowed paper with dark photocopier edges, the tool judged pages
1 and 3 to be 100% unreadable. Its rule for that case is to remove the whole page's content. The
held-back copy (made later with `--keep-held-back`) confirms it: pages 1 and 3 are flat grey, with
only the made-up replacement words floating where the originals were. On the upside-down page 2,
some stand-ins are random letters ("636 Jlntwca Tlpzjb, Hxbgsqr", "aafu.jhew15@ygmukqp.cks").

Logos were treated as photos: the GP letter's round clinic logo became a grey person silhouette, and
a letterhead line in the car-accident bundle was covered by a "photo" stand-in.

**Why it happens:** the tinted paper, colour speckle and dark page edges of these scans count as ink.
**What to change:** measure ink relative to the paper's own colour, ignore the outer page margin and
punch holes, and require stamp candidates to be clearly coloured (red or blue) and reasonably large.
Treat a coloured emblem in a letterhead as a logo, not a photo.

### 6. The stand-ins don't always read naturally

None of these leak anything, but a reader would notice them:

- **One doctor, four names:** the psychiatrist became "Margaret Santos", "M. Santos", "P. Jennifer
  Kavanagh" and "P. Jennifer Khan". The inconsistent ones are where the name is joined to a dash or
  the word "Page" in the footer.
- **Law firms became people:** "BENNETT LOWE LAWYERS" became "ELIZABETH WILLIAM SHERIDAN" and "Carter Webb
  Lawyers" became "Sarah Lauren Kowalski".
- **Impossible or mismatched details:** a made-up phone number with an area code that doesn't exist,
  "(39)"; email stand-ins that don't match the person's stand-in name.
- **Clipped edges:** replacements sometimes nick neighbouring letters ("Please", "with", "date") and
  stand-in signatures clip the "Kind regards" / "Yours faithfully" line above them.
- **Uneven text size:** replacement text is sometimes much smaller than the text around it.

### 7. Smaller notes

- Every run prints a harmless error from Python's clean-up step when it exits. It looks alarming in
  logs but doesn't affect the output.
- The fixed start-up cost is about 15 seconds per document. For many documents it would be cheaper
  to keep the tool running (the API server does this) than to start it per document.

## In short

- **Safe to show in the demo:** the GP referral letter. It's clean: nothing personal left, and the
  medical content, dates and suburbs are intact apart from one medicine name. The held-back behaviour
  is also worth showing as a feature ("when it isn't sure, it refuses to release"), using the IME
  report.
- **Avoid in the demo:** colour or aged scans (punched holes, changed checkboxes, pages that would be
  wiped), the solicitor's letter (two organisation names leak), and the psychiatric and car-accident
  documents (held back).
- **Top three fixes, by impact:**
  1. **Catch the missed organisation names and nicknames** (issue 2). These are the only real
     privacy leaks.
  2. **Stop the false alarms** that hold documents back (issue 1). Three small fixes would let three of
     the six documents through. One of them, keeping suburbs and postcodes out of replaced addresses
     (issue 3), also fixes a policy breach seen in four documents.
  3. **Stop colour and aged scans from triggering false stamps and page wipes** (issue 5). This is
     the biggest cause of visible damage.

## How this was measured, and its limits

- **Released documents:** each redacted PDF was read back by two independent text readers. A value
  counts as leaked if either reader still finds it; values neither reader could read even in the
  original are left out. Picture areas are judged from the tool's own record of what it treated and
  where, not from the pixels.
- **Held-back documents:** at the time of the run, the tool deleted the redacted copy when it refused
  to release, so these results come from the tool's sealed evaluator record. It shows what the tool
  found and what it would have replaced each detail with, but not the pixels. "Would have leaked"
  means the tool read the value and didn't mark it for replacement. A `--keep-held-back` switch was
  added afterwards; the held-back copies it produced are in
  `backend/out/synth_runs_ec2_2026-10-20/heldback/`, and the tool's records for them are identical to
  this run's.
- **The test set is synthetic.** The documents imitate the kinds of files Joel handles, but their
  scan damage (colour speckle, yellowing, copier edges) is generated, so the exact counts in issue 5
  may differ on real scans. The patterns are what matter.
- **Not yet done:** the same six documents through the `redaction_benchmark` project, which is being
  set up separately. It measures output pixels and the text layer more forensically, and splits
  failures by stage.
